## Unit Driving Question
How do we find the value — or values — that make a relationship true, and what happens when two conditions must be true at once?

## The mathematical story
An equation is a **question in disguise**: "what value makes this statement true?" Solving is not a ritual of moving symbols; it is **undoing, while keeping the balance** — whatever you do to one side you do to the other, because the two sides are genuinely equal. A single linear equation has (usually) exactly **one** solution, and you can always **check** it by substituting back — the answer either makes the statement true or it does not. Once a learner can solve, **forming** an equation turns a real situation into that same question: name the unknown, write what the situation says, solve, check against the story.

Then comes the unit's key widening. A relationship between **two** unknowns — like y = 2x + 1 — is not answered by a single value but by a **whole set** of (x, y) pairs, and the picture of that set is a **straight line**: every point on the line is a solution, and every solution is a point on the line. So a line *is* a set of solutions. Put **two** such relationships together and ask for the pair that satisfies *both* — that is a **simultaneous** problem, and its answer is the **common solution**: the single point where the two lines **cross**. Reading that intersection off a graph makes the idea concrete; **substitution** and **elimination** are two algebraic ways to find the same crossing exactly, without drawing.

The unit then loosens "equals" into "at least" and "at most". An **inequality** does not pin down one value; its solution is a **range** — a stretch of the number line, drawn with open or closed ends — and, for two variables, a **region** of the plane bounded by a line. That region is the same idea the Constructions unit met as a **locus**: the set of all points obeying a condition. Throughout, **checking by substitution** is the honest thread — a solution is only a solution if it makes the statement true.

**Arc:** solve one linear equation by keeping the balance → harder forms (both sides, brackets, fractions) → form an equation from a situation → a line is the set of all solutions (solve graphically) → two lines meet at the common solution (simultaneous, graphically) → find that meeting by substitution → find it by elimination → form and solve a pair in context → loosen equals to inequalities on a number line → inequalities as regions on a graph → consolidate, always checking.

This is Intermediate algebra's move from **one value** to **sets, pairs and regions of solutions** — the same reasoning-first stance the earlier units took, now applied to *what makes a relationship true and where those truths live*.

## Stepping Stones
Solve **linear equations** — including two-step, unknowns on both sides, brackets and fractions — by inverse operations that keep the equation balanced · **form** a linear equation from a worded or geometric situation, then solve and interpret it · understand a linear relationship in two variables as a **set of solutions** shown by a straight line · solve a pair of **simultaneous linear equations** by reading the **intersection** of their graphs, by **substitution**, and by **elimination**, and interpret the intersection as the **common solution** · **form and solve** a pair of simultaneous equations from context · solve **linear inequalities** and **represent** their solutions on a number line (open/closed ends) · **represent** the solution of an inequality (or two) as a shaded **region** on a graph · **check** every solution by substituting back into the original statement.

## Cornerstone emphasis across the unit
The spine is **Movement, Reflection and Connection**, with **Creativity** anchoring the forming-and-posing lessons. **Movement** is central and literal in the solving lessons — doing the same thing to both sides, collecting terms, dragging a line, removing an unknown — this is where algebra stops being symbol-pushing and becomes an action the learner controls and can justify. **Reflection** carries the deep ideas: *why* balancing preserves truth, what it means for a **line to be a set of solutions**, why an **intersection is the common solution**, and how a solution becomes a **range or region**. **Connection** binds the three faces of a relationship — the equation, its graph, and the situation it models — and links the graphical intersection to the algebraic methods that find it. **Creativity** anchors EQ-04 and EQ-09, where learners **form** their own equations and pairs. **Rest** anchors the mid-unit checkpoint and the EQ-11 consolidation.

### Nutrition — predominantly Mode B (intellectual), with honest Mode A where a context genuinely lives there
Per GreenPrint D6, Mode A (contextual) is reserved for mathematics that genuinely lives in food, hydration, body, health, sustainability or budgeting. Most of this unit is abstract algebra, so it must **not** dress ordinary word problems up as the Nutrition Cornerstone. Across EQ-01–EQ-03, EQ-05–EQ-08 and EQ-10, the honest mode is **Mode B — intellectual**: the satisfaction of a solution you can **check and trust**; the power of solving **two conditions at once**; and the elegance of algebra finding *exactly* the point a graph only shows approximately. **EQ-04, EQ-09 and EQ-11 may use Mode A honestly** where a forming/context problem genuinely lives in those domains — for example a **budgeting** pair ("two lunches and a drink cost £…, three lunches and two drinks cost £…"), a **hydration or mixing** ratio, or a **planting/sustainability** area constraint. Where a context is money-generic or purely mechanical, it is used honestly as a **problem setting**, but the Nutrition panel stays **Mode B**.
