# NEO Mathematics — Unit Blueprint: Circle Theorems (The Circle's Hidden Rules)
**Pathway:** Intermediate (internal stage code Y10) · **Term:** Autumn, Half-Term 2 — second of two HT2 units (paired with Equations, Inequalities & Simultaneous Relationships).
**Shape:** 10 lessons · 45 minutes each · with the Equations unit (11 lessons) this fills the 21-lesson Half-Term 2 (3 lessons/week × 7 weeks). *(May flex to 9 or 11 — decided live from evidence, per the responsive envelope.)*
**Qualification overlay:** Pearson Edexcel International GCSE (4MA1) Geometry — circle properties and **circle theorems**: parts of a circle; the angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference; the angle in a semicircle is 90°; angles in the same segment are equal; opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral sum to 180°; a tangent is perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact; the two tangents from an external point are equal; the alternate segment theorem; the perpendicular from the centre to a chord bisects the chord. Learners must **use the theorems to find angles and lengths, give reasons, and prove results.** **Out of scope for this unit:** intersecting-chord / secant-tangent length theorems (power of a point) and the formal circle equation x² + y² = r² — flagged as later Intermediate/Advanced work.
**Strand:** Geometry — builds on **2D & 3D Shapes** (parts of a circle, circumference, angle facts in polygons), the **angle toolkit** (angles on a line, around a point, in a triangle, isosceles base angles), and the **Constructions, Loci & Bearings** unit (the Dynamic Geometry Studio, compasses, perpendiculars).
**Status (v0.1):** blueprint framed; lessons to be built and reviewed one at a time, reusing the Dynamic Geometry Studio and a small new reusable **Circle Theorem Lab** interactive.

## Unit Driving Question
A circle looks featureless — so where do all its fixed angle rules come from, and how do I use and prove them?

## The mathematical story
A circle is the most symmetric shape there is, and at first glance the emptiest: no corners, no sides, nothing to grip. Yet the moment you place a few points on it and draw the lines between them — **chords**, **radii**, **tangents** — the circle turns out to be quietly full of **fixed relationships**. Drag a point around the edge and an angle stubbornly refuses to change; the angle at the **centre** is always exactly **double** the angle at the **circumference** standing on the same arc; a tangent always meets the radius **square-on**. These are not coincidences to memorise but **invariants to notice, then justify**.

The unit is built as a sequence of such discoveries. Each theorem is met the same way: **see the invariant** (drag points in the Studio / Circle Theorem Lab and watch a measured angle hold steady or lock to a ratio), **state it precisely**, **justify it** (almost every proof rests on one humble fact — two radii make an **isosceles triangle**, so the base angles are equal), then **use it** to find unknown angles and lengths. The angle-at-the-centre theorem is the keystone: the semicircle right angle, the equal angles in the same segment, and the cyclic-quadrilateral rule all fall out of it. The tangent results and the alternate segment theorem add the second family, about lines that touch rather than cross.

The unit then turns from single theorems to **combining** them — real circle problems rarely use one rule in isolation, so learners choose which theorems apply, chain them, and **give a reason at every step**. It closes on **reasoning and proof**: deriving a theorem from more basic facts, and solving rich multi-step problems. The reasoning-first stance is unavoidable here and central: a circle-theorem answer without its reasons is only half an answer.

**Arc:** name the parts → the angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference (the keystone) → the semicircle right angle (special case) → angles in the same segment → cyclic quadrilaterals → tangent–radius and equal tangents → the alternate segment theorem → chords and the perpendicular from the centre → combine several theorems with reasons → reason, prove and solve.

This is Intermediate geometry's move from **measuring shapes** to **deducing hidden structure and proving it** — the same reasoning-first stance the earlier units took, now applied to the richest single figure on the syllabus.

## Stepping Stones
Name and identify the **parts of a circle** — centre, radius, diameter, chord, arc, sector, segment, tangent, and the arc an angle *stands on* · know and use that the **angle at the centre is twice the angle at the circumference** on the same arc · know and use that the **angle in a semicircle is 90°** · know and use that **angles in the same segment are equal** · know and use that **opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral sum to 180°** · know and use that a **tangent is perpendicular to the radius** at the point of contact, and that the **two tangents from an external point are equal** · know and use the **alternate segment theorem** · know and use that the **perpendicular from the centre to a chord bisects the chord** · **combine** two or more theorems to find angles and lengths, **stating a reason at each step** · **prove** a circle theorem from more basic angle facts.

## Cornerstone emphasis across the unit
The spine is **Reflection and Movement**, with **Connection** framing and **Creativity** anchoring the proof work. **Reflection** carries the heart of the unit — every theorem is a *noticed invariant justified*: why the centre angle doubles, why the semicircle gives a right angle, why opposite cyclic angles are supplementary, why the alternate segment angles match. **Movement** is literal and central in the discovery moments — **dragging** a point around the circle and watching a measured angle hold steady or lock to a ratio is what turns a stated rule into a felt certainty, and it is where a circle stops being featureless. **Connection** binds the figure together (parts, and how each theorem builds on the angle-at-the-centre keystone) and links the whole unit to the isosceles-triangle fact underneath nearly every proof. **Creativity** anchors CT-10, where learners **prove** a theorem and pose/solve their own multi-step problems. **Rest** anchors the mid-unit checkpoint and the CT-10 consolidation.

### Nutrition — predominantly Mode B (intellectual), with honest Mode A only where a context genuinely lives there
Per GreenPrint D6, Mode A (contextual) is reserved for mathematics that genuinely lives in food, hydration, body, health, sustainability or budgeting. Circle theorems are abstract deductive geometry, so this unit is **predominantly Mode B — intellectual**: the quiet astonishment of an angle that will not change however you drag it; the elegance of a whole family of results falling out of one keystone theorem; the satisfaction of a proof that closes with certainty; the power of a reason at every step. Where a genuine context exists it may be used **honestly as a problem setting** (circles are everywhere in **design, engineering and navigation** — a bridge arch, a camera's field of view, a cog, a Ferris wheel, the "safe" angle a tangent sight-line makes), but the Nutrition panel stays **Mode B** unless a task genuinely lives in a D6 domain; circle geometry is not dressed up as nutrition.

## Reconnection Routes used
Angles on a straight line, around a point, and in a triangle (KS3 angle toolkit) · **isosceles-triangle base angles** (the single most-used fact in circle proofs — two radii always make an isosceles triangle) · the exterior-angle-of-a-triangle fact · parts of a circle and circumference (from the **2D & 3D Shapes** unit) · quadrilateral angle sum (360°) · the **Dynamic Geometry Studio** and perpendicular-bisector construction (from **Constructions, Loci & Bearings**) · Pythagoras' theorem (for chord-length problems in CT-08). Several routes are secure from earlier units; the isosceles-base-angle route is load-bearing across the whole unit and should be offered early and often.

## Resource reuse — audit before building
- **Dynamic Geometry Studio (CT-01, CT-06, CT-08, CT-10):** the shared self-contained construction environment (already built and published to `docs/tools/geometry-studio.html`) is the right tool for learner-driven work — constructing a tangent, dropping a perpendicular from the centre to a chord, verifying a theorem by their own construction. **Reuse it and add circle-theorem tasks to the inline `TASKS` registry**, launched with the established in-lesson "Open in the Geometry Studio →" cards and guarded by `validate_studio_links()`. **Build dependency — resolved:** a **Measure → Angle** tool (select three points arm–vertex–arm; reads the non-reflex angle at the vertex) is **already built and wired** in the Studio (`data-act="mangle"` → `measang`), verified correct (90°/45°/120°). It gives the non-reflex angle (≤180°), which is the standard circle-theorem case; for a reflex centre angle (major arc) learners set up the minor-arc case. No Studio change is required before building.
- **New reusable "Circle Theorem Lab" (CT-02–05, CT-07, CT-09):** the *discovery* moments need a lighter, presettable inline component — a circle with **draggable points on the circumference** (dependent points, so they stay on the circle) and **live angle readouts** that update as you drag, so an invariant can be *seen* holding steady or locking to a ratio. No existing NEO interactive does this, so a **small new reusable component is warranted** — built once, preset per lesson (which points are draggable, which angles are read out, whether to show the 2:1 link, the diameter, the segment, the cyclic quad, or the tangent–chord pair). This is the circle-theorem analogue of the Straight Lines **Graph Lab** and the Quadratics **Parabola Lab**: build once, reuse across the unit. It complements the Studio (Lab = guided noticing; Studio = learner construction).
- **Angle-fact scaffolding:** reuse the established reasoning-first pattern — every angle a learner states must carry a **reason** (chosen from the theorem names), echoing the "give a reason" habit the qualification demands.
- **Scratchpad:** offer a plain and a **circle/dotted** background; circle-theorem working (adding construction lines, marking equal angles) suits a printed circle preset.

## Build approach
Frame one lesson → present a 3–5 option interactive bank (Stage 2) → build the full ecosystem (interactive, Practice Companion, NEO Maths Scratchpad, Learner + Supporting Adult guidance) → QA against the shipping gates **including QA gate 6a (equivalent-form answer acceptance — numeric equivalents, fractions/decimals, and spelled-out number words, per the canonical `num()` parser)** → review. One lesson at a time; never pre-build ahead of review. Reasoning is assessed, not just answers: every practice and final task requires the **theorem name / reason**, not only the number. British English, "learner" not "student", the six NEO Cornerstones in locked order, no chronological year labels in learner-facing text, timings as invitations, **precise mathematical vocabulary** (**centre, radius, diameter, chord, arc, sector, segment, tangent, subtend, cyclic, alternate segment** — glossed once on first use), honest Nutrition modes, and the reasoning-first standard that a circle-theorem result must be **justified with a reason**, not merely produced. After each accepted lesson, offer to update the live Intermediate Coverage Map (Stepping Stones, Reconnection Routes, status, evidence, header audit).

### Sequencing note
CT-02 (angle at the centre) is the **keystone** and must be secure before CT-03–05, which are its consequences — teach and check it thoroughly. The **isosceles-triangle base-angle** fact underpins almost every proof in the unit; surface it in CT-01/CT-02 and keep a Reconnection Route to it available throughout. Order within the tangent/chord family (CT-06–08) is flexible, but the **alternate segment theorem (CT-07)** is the hardest single idea in the unit and should not be rushed or combined.
